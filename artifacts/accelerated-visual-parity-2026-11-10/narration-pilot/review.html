Approved C01 text

What evidence would make that choice responsible? What data did you put into the tool to get there? A beautiful page can hide those questions for a little while. It cannot answer them. In the opening of Accelerated, I start with that blinking cursor because it is familiar. The book begins with product teams carrying noise, conflicting priorities, and documents that do not move decisions. The work here is to give the team a way to learn, choose, and keep its judgment when the page fills up. A product system sounds larger than it needs to be. Think about the last idea that arrived in a meeting. Someone noticed a customer problem. Someone else had a competing story. A third person asked for a number nobody had. The team left with another document to write. A system gives those moments a path. It helps you record the signal, name the decision, state what you believe, and come back to what happened. AI can help organize messy input, test the shape of an argument, and make a first draft. The judgment still belongs to people who understand the consequences. That is the argument in the first chapter. I describe AI as a strategy partner, a signal amplifier, and a structure builder. Those are jobs it may help with, under the right conditions. They are not permission to trust every output. If it gives you five polished options, you still need to ask who benefits, what is missing, and which option you can test safely. Speed can create room to think. It can also fill that room with more noise. Look at this map. Signals. Alignment. Momentum. It is a way to navigate the workshop. First, take the raw material coming at you and find a question worth answering. Then give the people carrying the decision a shared direction. Then build a rhythm that lets the next round of evidence change the plan. The book organizes its printed chapters as Systems, Align, and Momentum. The workshop uses the three labels on this existing infographic as a visual guide. You will see where those paths cross; the graphic is not a replacement for the chapters. In these first lessons we stay close to the signal side. We will choose one decision, work backward to evidence, and ask what a small test could teach us. There is a human thread through the book, too: Sam, a product manager who has talent but keeps getting pulled into reactive work.

Approved C02 text

Before you try any prompt, decide what information can leave your desk. The book's data-safety note comes before the chapters for a reason. Customer details, finances, internal strategy, and company records can carry obligations that a convenient text box does not erase. My working rule for this course is simple: use public information, material your organization has approved for that use, or a made-up example. If you are unsure, stop and check the policy that governs your work. Do not put restricted material into an external model to make the worksheet feel realistic. We can learn the method with a synthetic case. Imagine a team trying to improve the first week for new members. We can invent a harmless request list and test our reasoning without using anyone's account, message, or history. On the screen, watch the example move into a three-column boundary: public, approved internal, restricted. “Approved internal” means approved for this particular tool and purpose, not merely available to you at work. This boundary follows the decision file through every lesson. It is part of product judgment, not an appendix. Let's make the first page of your decision file.
