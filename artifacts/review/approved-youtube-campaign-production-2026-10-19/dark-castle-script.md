# I Rebuilt the Chess Lesson Around the Decision

## 0:00–1:00 · The move and the reason

Two players can make the same move. One saw the threat. The other guessed. A board that only celebrates the move treats those two moments as equal. That bothered me while I was building Dark Castle Chess. I wanted the lesson to live in the choice, and to survive what happens next. In a moment, I will show you three real product screens: the position in Explore, the handoff into computer play, and the response with coaching. Watch one question travel through all three: what did the player notice before moving? I am not asking a game to read anyone's mind. I am asking the product to keep the position and its consequence visible long enough for a person to learn from them. Start with this board. Pause before the move. What would you protect, and what would you risk?

## 1:00–2:00 · The position

Explore is where the player can inspect a position before adding the pressure of a computer reply. The public gameplay capture shows the board in that learning state. This is where I want a player to slow down. A piece can be moved for many reasons: a threat, an opening, an escape, or a hope that the opponent does not see something. The screen can show the available position. It cannot prove why a person chose a square. So the lesson begins with a prompt to look, choose, and explain. I would ask a learner to name the piece they think is in danger, then point to the square that changes that danger. That small act gives a coach, a parent, or the learner a way to discuss a decision instead of praising a result alone. Now keep that same position in mind. The next screen is the test.

## 2:00–3:00 · The handoff

When the player moves from exploration into computer play, a reset would break the thread of the lesson. The second public capture shows the transfer into play. The important design choice is continuity: the position being studied must still be the position that gets answered. Otherwise the tutorial becomes a separate room with no doorway into the game. I have seen learning tools do that. They explain one thing beautifully, then send the learner somewhere else and ask them to reconstruct the context alone. I wanted Dark Castle Chess to carry more of that burden. Compare these two screens. Look at the board state and at the action that starts the response. If the state has carried through, the player can test the reason they gave a moment ago. If it has not, even a polished coaching message arrives too late to connect to the choice.

## 3:00–4:00 · The response

Now the computer answers. The third public capture shows the response with coaching and notation. Here is where the original reason meets a consequence. Maybe the threat was real. Maybe another piece became exposed. Maybe the move was legal and still missed the stronger idea. The screen offers material for reflection, but I want to keep the claim precise: these captures show product behavior, not measured improvement in chess skill. A useful next question is, what changed after the reply? Point to the board before reaching for the label on the move. Then read the coaching and notation as an explanation you can test against what is visible. That order matters. A confident label can stop a learner from looking. A visible consequence invites them to look again. This is the first payoff: the move is no longer an isolated event.

## 4:00–5:00 · A small learning loop

The loop is simple enough to remember: inspect, choose, meet the response, explain what changed. I designed the sequence around that rhythm because learning should not depend on the player remembering a detached lesson five minutes later. The position is the shared object. The decision is the hinge. The reply gives the learner another chance to inspect their reasoning. Notice that I am not promising the game will always know the best explanation for every player. A computer can calculate positions; teaching still asks for judgment about what a person needs to understand. That is why I want the product to make its evidence available to a human conversation. A parent can ask what the player expected. A coach can ask what surprised them. The learner can compare their first explanation with the board after the reply.

## 5:00–6:00 · What a completion screen misses

Imagine a lesson that ends with a green check and a score. The player may feel good, and sometimes that is enough to keep going. But the check does not tell us whether the next unfamiliar position will make sense. When I look at the Dark Castle sequence, I see a better unit of work: one decision with a before and an after. It is small, but it is inspectable. We can ask where the player's attention went, what the reply revealed, and whether the explanation would still make sense if the pieces changed. This is also a product lesson beyond chess. If you build a learning experience, keep the evidence of the user's decision close to the feedback. Give people a path back to the moment where they chose. The more context they must reconstruct, the more work the product has quietly handed back to them.

## 6:00–7:00 · The boundary

There is a boundary I want to say out loud. These public gameplay screens show an Explore position, transfer into play, and a computer response with coaching. They do not show a controlled study, long term retention, or a measured gain in skill. I will not ask the images to carry a claim they cannot carry. What they can show is the architecture of a lesson: the same decision remains visible while the player meets its consequence. That is a design hypothesis worth testing with real learners. I would watch whether they can explain the move before and after the reply, and whether the second explanation grows more precise. I would also watch where they get lost. A beautiful board does not remove confusion by itself. The questions around the board decide whether the experience teaches.

## 7:00–8:00 · Payoff and invitation

Return to the first position. Two players made the same move. The product cannot declare that they learned the same thing. It can make the reasoning easier to see and easier to revisit. That is why I rebuilt this lesson around the decision. The board, the handoff, and the reply form one thread a learner can follow. Completion tells us a move happened. A visible decision and its consequence give us something to discuss, challenge, and remember. If you teach chess, build learning tools, or learn by playing, try this with one position: ask for the reason before the move, then ask what changed after the answer.
