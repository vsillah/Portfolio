# The AI Cost That Never Appears on the Invoice

## 0:00–1:00 · The missing line

The invoice says twenty dollars a month. Then someone spends an hour checking the answer, another hour fixing a handoff, and a manager has to decide who owns the mistake. Those hours never appear on the software bill. I built the AI Audit Calculator because a price tag is a poor map of the work surrounding a tool. In this video I will walk through the real calculator screen and a practical way to count the work that moves, grows, or disappears when AI enters a workflow. Keep one question with you: who owns the cleanup when the answer is wrong? By the end, you will have a way to ask that question before signing up for another tool. Start with a simple task your team already does. Name the person who receives the output. Now follow the work after the output arrives.

## 1:00–2:00 · A workflow, not a license

Most buying conversations start with seats and subscription tiers. That is easy to compare. The hard part is tracing one job from request to accepted result. Suppose a team uses AI to draft a response. Someone supplies context. Someone reviews accuracy and tone. Someone corrects missing facts. Someone sends the final version and answers for its consequences. If the tool removes twenty minutes of drafting but adds thirty minutes of review, the monthly fee tells only a small part of the story. Before running a calculator, I would draw that path on paper. Mark the hands that touch the work and the points where it waits. Do it with one real workflow, not a vague promise that AI will make everyone faster. The first pattern interrupt is a blank line on the invoice. Write the review time there. The economics look different immediately.

## 3:00–4:00 · The four costs

I look for four kinds of cost around an AI workflow. First is the visible bill: seats, usage, and integrations. Second is review: the human time required to decide whether an answer is safe and useful. Third is repair: rework after the output misses context, breaks a process, or reaches the wrong person. Fourth is coordination: the time spent clarifying ownership, exceptions, and handoffs. Those categories are a practical checklist, not a universal accounting standard. A small team may feel the coordination cost most sharply because the same person often wears every hat. A larger team may hide it across departments. Either way, the question is concrete: when the tool answers, what work begins? Write that work down before claiming savings. The useful comparison is the same task with and without the tool, measured to an accepted result.

## 4:00–5:00 · A one-task audit

Pick one recurring task and give it a name. Count how often it happens in a month. Record the minutes spent preparing input, reviewing output, correcting errors, and handing the result to the next person. Then record those same steps in the existing process. Keep the quality bar equal. If one path creates a fast draft that nobody can use, it has not finished the job. If an experienced worker quietly fixes every answer, include that work. The calculator's assessment can help frame the decision, but the inputs must come from observation. This is where I would ask two people to walk the same job: the person buying the tool and the person cleaning up after it. Their accounts may differ. That difference is evidence worth investigating, not a nuisance to average away. The hidden cost often sits between those two views.

## 5:00–6:00 · Who owns the exception?

Now change one condition. The source information is incomplete, or a request falls outside the normal rule. Where does the AI output go? If the system has no named owner for exceptions, the cleanup becomes everybody's problem and nobody's measured work. In an audit, I want to see a decision path: who can accept, revise, refuse, or escalate the result? How do they know which choice is theirs? Where is the record when someone asks what happened? This is a governance question with a cost attached. It affects trust, time, and sometimes the dignity of a customer or colleague who has to repeat the same facts. A useful AI system should make the next responsible action clearer. If it makes the exception harder to find, the low monthly price is misleading.

## 6:00–7:00 · From score to action

A score can focus attention, but it cannot manage the change for you. Look at the calculator's action guidance as a starting point for a small test. Choose one workflow. Assign an owner. Decide what an acceptable output looks like. Track preparation, review, repair, and escalation for a bounded period. Then compare that evidence with the current process. If the tool helps, keep the part that helps and write down the conditions under which it works. If the tool adds work, say so. That answer can save the team more than a flattering dashboard. I built the calculator to make these conversations less vague. The people doing the work already know where the friction lives. The audit should give them a place to name it and a way to test whether a proposed fix actually reduces it.

## 7:00–8:00 · The decision

Return to the invoice. The subscription price still matters. It is simply one line in a larger operating bill. The team also pays with review time, repair, coordination, and the attention of people asked to catch what the system misses.
