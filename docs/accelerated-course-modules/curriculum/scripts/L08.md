# L08 · Metrics and the ten-part learning plan

**Target:** 12:00. **Promise:** Write a test that can change course when results arrive. **Decision-file output:** Ten-component learning plan and metric definition. **Voice:** exact about what a measure can say; plain about what remains unknown. **Source key:** canonical EPUB `c015.xhtml` Chapter 12, “Build a Metrics Ladder,” “Real Story: The Dashboard That Looked Great While We Failed,” “Tie Metrics to Hypotheses, Not Just Outputs,” “Set Triggers—And Prepare to Be Wrong,” “Beware of Destructive Metric Pairs,” “Balance Quantitative with Qualitative”; `c016.xhtml` Chapter 13, “The Ten Components,” “Final Point.” Book examples and figures are not current verified product evidence.

## Scene 1 · 00:00–02:00 · A dashboard can look healthy
**Source:** Ch12, “Real Story: The Dashboard That Looked Great While We Failed,” “Build a Metrics Ladder” (`c015.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A01, 00:00–00:35. **B-roll:** synthetic dashboard shows rising activity while the task-completion question remains unanswered; no actual analytics capture. **Delivery:** Give the audience time to see the misleading relationship.

**Visible copy:** `Activity is not the customer outcome`.
```narration
The dashboard is green. More people are opening the page. The team celebrates, then support hears that members still cannot finish the first task. A rising activity line did not answer the question we were trying to solve. Chapter Twelve tells a version of this failure: a dashboard looked healthy while the product was missing the customer outcome. The point is to choose a measure that belongs to the decision. In the membership service example, the business may care about sustained participation. A member cares about getting started without confusion. The team can first observe whether a person identifies and begins the intended action. Those are different levels of the metrics ladder in the book: business value, customer value, and near-term traction. The lower step does not automatically cause the upper one. We need to test the connection. If page opens rise while people still ask staff what to do, the green chart is a warning, not a win. Write the customer behavior in a sentence before selecting a metric. For this lesson, it is: a new member understands the first task and starts it without translation from staff. Now ask what observation would support or challenge that sentence. A useful measure can be counted or described consistently. It also needs a definition that another person can apply to the same event. We will put that definition into the learning plan before seeing any result.
```

## Scene 2 · 02:00–04:00 · Define the measure and its guardrail
**Source:** Ch12, “Tie Metrics to Hypotheses, Not Just Outputs,” “Beware of Destructive Metric Pairs,” “Balance Quantitative with Qualitative” (`c015.xhtml`). **Avatar:** off. **B-roll:** synthetic metric-definition card with numerator, denominator, observation method, and qualitative companion; paired charts show opens versus successful starts. **Delivery:** Keep the math concrete and the limits audible.

**Visible copy:** `Define a successful start`.
```narration
Define a successful start before the test begins. For a moderated session, that might mean the participant can point to the intended action, explain its purpose in their own words, and begin without staff explaining the instruction. The numerator is the number of observed participants who meet that definition. The denominator is the number of eligible participants who reach the first-task moment. Record exclusions before the session, such as an interrupted test or a technical outage. A second observer should be able to check the same notes and understand the count. That measure is close to the behavior we seek. It still cannot tell us everything. Ask the participant where they hesitated and what they expected to happen. That qualitative note may show whether the copy, confirmation, or task itself is the problem. Chapter Twelve also warns about destructive metric pairs. We could maximize time spent on the first task while saying we want people to begin quickly. Longer time might mean careful engagement, or it might mean confusion. We need the context before celebrating either direction. Pair the successful-start measure with a guardrail: the person should not have to ask staff to rescue the flow or repeat an action because the state is unclear. A planned measurement cannot promise a conversion or retention lift. The definition is a proposed measurement instrument; the outcome remains unknown until a test is run and reviewed.
```

## Scene 3 · 04:00–06:00 · The first five components establish the bet
**Source:** Ch13, “The Ten Components” (`c016.xhtml`); Ch12, “Build a Metrics Ladder” (`c015.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A02, 04:00–04:25. **B-roll:** book-derived worksheet fields 1–5 appear in exact EPUB order, one at a time; no invented framework icons. **Delivery:** Say the field names clearly, then connect each to the example.

**Visible copy:** `1–5 · problem / cohort / hypothesis / scope / metrics`.
```narration
Chapter Thirteen gives the learning plan ten components. The first five establish what we are testing. One: business problem and customer outcome. Our example names the friction at the first task and the member's need to begin with confidence. We will not claim a business lift from this small exercise. Two: target persona or cohort. Use the living persona card from lesson four: a new member at the first-task moment. Add the source and the uncertainty so the card stays honest about what we know. Three: hypothesis statement. We believe a clearer first-task card, for a new member in that moment, will help them identify and begin the intended action without staff translation. Four: experiment scope. Specify the test setting, who is eligible, how long observation lasts, and which version of the card they see. Exclude the reminder system and the rest of onboarding. Five: metrics and success criteria. Use the successful-start definition we just wrote, the qualitative hesitation notes, and a threshold set by the actual study owner before data arrives. These fields belong together. If the cohort is vague, a clean metric can mislead. If the outcome is vague, a detailed experiment may only test whether a screen is clickable. On this worksheet, leave the actual threshold marked for owner decision. That blank is honest. The team must fill it before running a real test; it must not choose a convenient number after looking at results.
```

## Scene 4 · 06:00–08:00 · The next five decide what happens afterward
**Source:** Ch13, “The Ten Components,” “Final Point” (`c016.xhtml`); Ch12, “Set Triggers—And Prepare to Be Wrong” (`c015.xhtml`). **Avatar:** off. **B-roll:** worksheet fields 6–10 appear in exact EPUB order; one simple branch shows continue, revise, or stop. **Delivery:** Pause slightly at the tripwire because it must be chosen before results.

**Visible copy:** `6–10 · timing / tripwires / pivots / future path / backlog`.
```narration
The next five components keep the test from ending at a chart. Six: timing and review strategy. Decide when the team checks for setup problems, when it reads the observations, and when it makes the next decision. Seven: triggers and tripwires. State what pattern would support continuing, what would require revision, and what would make the team stop or investigate harm. For our example, repeated confusion or unclear failure states would pause expansion even if some people begin. The exact numerical threshold is still an owner decision before the test. Eight: pivot options. If members understand the card but never return, investigate the reminder path. If they reach the card but cannot tell whether the action worked, test confirmation. Those are possible branches, not findings. Nine: future state path. If the small test supports the hypothesis and passes its guardrail, name the next bounded question rather than announcing a full rollout. Perhaps the team tests the same instruction with another eligible group. Ten: hypothesis backlog. Save the original belief, result, learning, owner, and date so the next team does not start from an empty page. That is the book's order. The plan carries both a decision and a memory of how the decision was made. A result can be positive, negative, or inconclusive. The point is that each route has a responsible next action already visible before the room gets attached to one answer.
```

## Scene 5 · 08:00–10:00 · Walk the plan against two possible results
**Source:** Ch12, “Tie Metrics to Hypotheses, Not Just Outputs,” “Set Triggers—And Prepare to Be Wrong,” “Balance Quantitative with Qualitative” (`c015.xhtml`); Ch13, “The Ten Components” (`c016.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A03, 08:00–08:25. **B-roll:** synthetic result cards marked as possibilities in protected production notes; on-screen audience copy says “If A” and “If B,” not fake measured data. **Delivery:** Speak conditionally throughout.

**Visible copy:** `If A → next question · If B → next question`.
```narration
Before there is any data, walk the plan through two possible results. In the first, participants understand the action and begin, but several say the confirmation is unclear. The successful-start measure may look promising. The guardrail tells us the work is not finished. We record the uncertainty and test feedback after the action before broadening the scope. In the second, participants cannot explain why the task matters. That result challenges the instruction itself. The team revises the purpose statement and tests again. These are imagined branches. Both results are possibilities, not findings. Notice how the metric definition changes the conversation. “More opens” would let both situations hide behind activity. “Understands and begins without translation” brings the actual behavior into view. The qualitative note explains why a count moved and what it cannot tell us. If the sample is too small, unrepresentative, or interrupted, mark the outcome inconclusive and choose a better test. Chapter Twelve asks us to tie measures to hypotheses and prepare to be wrong. Chapter Thirteen gives us a place to record that preparation. The worksheet is useful only if a team can follow it when the answer is inconvenient. Keep the threshold, exclusions, and branch choices dated before the test. Then, after a real review, record what happened and what the team decided. Do not rewrite the original bet to make the result look inevitable afterward to anyone.
```
