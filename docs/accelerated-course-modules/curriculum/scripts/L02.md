# L02 · Prioritize before generating

**Target:** 10:00. **Promise:** Narrow a crowded request list to one decision worth testing. **Decision-file output:** One-page priority decision with two rejected alternatives and reasons. **Voice:** candid, measured; the synthetic option sheet is a teaching device. **Source key:** canonical EPUB `c005.xhtml` Chapter 2, “The Trap That Catches Good Teams,” “Think of It Like Airport Signage,” “Simplicity Is About Better”; `c006.xhtml` Chapter 3, “From Brainstorming to Case Structuring,” “Match Your Framework to Your Question,” “Layer in Business Model Options,” “The Business Model Decision Tree.” The book's numerical claims and client outcomes are deliberately omitted because no independent evidence packet accompanies them.

## Scene 1 · 00:00–01:30 · Fifteen priorities
**Source:** Ch2 opening and “The Trap That Catches Good Teams” (`c005.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A01, 00:00–00:30. **B-roll:** synthetic planning board, not a real company meeting. **Delivery:** Let “fifteen top priorities” land as the absurdity.

```narration
The meeting has barely started and the board is full. Fifteen roadmap items. Fifteen top priorities. The person asking for retention has a good reason. So does the person asking for revenue. The refactor matters to the engineers who will carry the system next quarter. Everybody can defend their note. That is why the room is stuck. In Chapter Two, I tell a version of this planning meeting and ask what the team cannot afford to get wrong. That question changes the shape of the conversation. The book's case lands on the first minutes of a new user's experience. We do not need to borrow that answer for every team. We need to borrow the move: step away from the list and name the consequence of a wrong choice. Open the decision file from Lesson One. Read the decision at the top. If it still sounds like “choose all the good ideas,” make it sharper. Whose experience or business result is at stake? What would a choice teach you before the next planning cycle?
```

## Scene 2 · 01:30–03:00 · What the list hides
**Source:** Ch2, “The Trap That Catches Good Teams,” “Think of It Like Airport Signage” (`c005.xhtml`). **Avatar:** off. **B-roll:** Signals plate with three synthetic requests revealed as separate cards. **Delivery:** Conversational; do not assert book statistics.

```narration
A long list can feel fair. Each person sees their concern represented. But the list does not tell you which problem to solve first, and it cannot tell you why. That is the trap in the chapter. More information may give every proposal a stronger defense while leaving the actual decision untouched. Think about airport signage. A terminal can offer plenty of services, yet the traveler needs to know where to go next. If the sign does not make that clear, more decoration will not help. The same thing happens in a product meeting. Before asking an AI tool for twenty feature ideas, ask what path the person is trying to find and what the team can change. Here is a synthetic case we will use on screen: a small membership service has three requests. Rewrite its welcome email. Add a guided first task. Build a dashboard for staff. They might all help. We do not know yet. Our job is to compare them against the same decision, rather than letting the loudest request become the plan.
```

## Scene 3 · 03:00–04:33 · Match the frame to the question
**Source:** Ch3, “From Brainstorming to Case Structuring,” “Match Your Framework to Your Question” (`c006.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A02, 03:00–03:20. **B-roll:** readable 3C + P cards; synthetic sheet. **Delivery:** Spell the framework once, then use everyday words.

```narration
Chapter Three gives us a way to structure a messy opportunity. The first move is to decide what question the case is answering. If you are asking whether a market is attractive, the book offers a market frame. If you are choosing a product or segment, it offers three C plus P: customer, company, competition, product. We are choosing a product move, so that second frame fits. Customer: which request responds to a real, urgent job? Company: what can this team actually deliver and maintain? Competition: how is the need handled now, even if the alternative is a spreadsheet or another person? Product: what is the smallest offer that could show value? Notice what this does to our synthetic options. The welcome email may be easy to ship, but we still need to know whether misunderstanding is the problem. The guided task might help people begin, but it could add burden. The staff dashboard may improve visibility for employees while leaving the member's first step untouched. We have turned three attractive artifacts into three questions that can be checked.
```

## Scene 4 · 04:33–06:06 · Compare without fake precision
**Source:** Ch3, “Match Your Framework to Your Question,” “Why This Matters for Innovation” (`c006.xhtml`); Ch2, “Simplicity Is About Better” (`c005.xhtml`). **Avatar:** off. **B-roll:** synthetic option table with qualitative notes, no invented score or customer claim. **Delivery:** Emphasize “unknown” without apology.

```narration
Now put the three options side by side. For each one, write the customer pain it might address, the business reason to care, the learning value of trying it, the risk, and whether you can reverse the choice. We do not have to invent a perfect numerical score. In fact, a precise score would be dishonest when the evidence is thin. On our sample sheet, the welcome email is low effort and easy to undo. Its weak point is that it assumes words are the barrier. The guided first task could expose whether people need a clearer starting point. Its weak point is that we do not know if they want guidance or a faster path. The staff dashboard might help the team see where members pause, but it asks for more build work before we have agreed what we need to observe. This is a hypothetical exercise. It is not a report from customers. You can disagree with my provisional ranking if you can name what evidence changes it. That is what a useful priority conversation sounds like.
```

## Scene 5 · 06:06–07:31 · Economic fit belongs in the case
**Source:** Ch3, “Layer in Business Model Options,” “The Business Model Decision Tree” (`c006.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A03, 06:06–06:26. **B-roll:** book decision-tree questions as clean text cards, not a screenshot of a live tool. **Delivery:** Slow at each question; avoid the book's unverified B2B result.

```narration
There is another question that can make a promising idea fail: how does the value reach a customer, and how does the work sustain itself? Chapter Three brings business model options into the case after the customer and product question is clear. It asks whether people need to experience value before paying, whether value accumulates over time, whether a free tier is affordable, and whether usage is predictable. Those are decision questions, not a menu that automatically picks your business. Our membership example may not need a new pricing model at all. But if the guided task changes who gets help and who pays for it, we should say so before we build. The book includes a B2B analytics case and names a tool called StratQuest. We are using neither as proof of a current product outcome here. What matters for this lesson is the discipline: first structure the case, then consider the economic fit. An AI brainstorm can offer possibilities. It cannot supply the missing customer and operating evidence by sounding certain.
```

## Scene 6 · 07:31–09:06 · Make a reversible choice
**Source:** Ch2, “The Trap That Catches Good Teams” (`c005.xhtml`); Ch3, “I Rest My Case (for now)” (`c006.xhtml`). **Avatar:** off. **B-roll:** decision-file option sheet; selected path receives a subtle outline, rejected paths remain readable. **Delivery:** Invite disagreement with the example.

```narration
For the sample case, I would choose a small test of the guided first task. I would not commit to a full guided onboarding product. I would show a simple first step to a few consenting participants through an approved research process, then ask whether it helped them begin. I would keep the welcome email as an alternative if the test shows the language is confusing. I would hold the staff dashboard until the team knows which question it needs the dashboard to answer. My reason is not that guided tasks always win. It is that this option can test the starting-point assumption while keeping the next commitment small. Your decision may go another way. Put your choice at the top of the page and write the sentence that makes it defensible: “We choose this because...” Then record the two alternatives you are setting aside. Give each one a real reason, not a vague “lower priority.” A decision file that only celebrates the winner loses half the learning. When new evidence arrives, those alternatives tell you what you thought you were trading away.
```

## Scene 7 · 09:06–10:00 · Handoff to discovery
**Source:** Ch3, “Match Your Framework to Your Question” (`c006.xhtml`); Ch2 opening (`c005.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A04, 09:35–10:00. **B-roll:** choice page folds into evidence-gap card for next lesson. **Delivery:** Soft close, direct to learner.

```narration
Pause here and finish the page. You need the chosen option, two alternatives, the reason for each, and one assumption that could overturn your choice. If you are working with a real team, keep sensitive inputs in the approved place. You can do this exercise with a fictional case and still practice the decision. When you come back, read your assumption out loud. Is it something you know, or something you hope? In the next lesson we will work backward from that sentence. We will ask what answer the decision owner needs and what evidence would change the conversation. The board of fifteen priorities will still exist tomorrow. You have done the harder thing today: made one choice clear enough to challenge.
```
