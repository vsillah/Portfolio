## Scene 1 · 00:00–01:40 · The quarter is full
**Source:** Ch10, “Anchor Roadmaps in Customer Outcomes,” “Make Learning the Only Guaranteed Deliverable” (`c013.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A01, 00:00–00:30. **B-roll:** synthetic quarter plan crowded with promised features; second column begins with one customer outcome. **Delivery:** Start in a planning room, not with a slogan.

**Visible copy:** `Delivery date + learning question`.
```narration
The quarter is full before it starts. A new first-task card is due in two weeks. A reminder system is penciled in after that. Someone has promised a dashboard by the end of the month. The schedule looks responsible, but the team still cannot answer a basic question: why do new members hesitate at the first task? Our feedback ledger gave us a possible explanation. The roadmap bet gave us a small test. Chapter Ten asks us to put the learning beside the delivery. Write the outcome we seek at the top: a member can understand and begin the first task without staff translating it. Under that, write what we need to learn first. Is the barrier unclear instruction, weak confirmation, or something else? A feature date can tell us when work will ship. A learning checkpoint tells us when we will inspect what the work taught us and decide whether to continue. Let us build two checkpoints for the membership service we have been following. We are planning the questions before any member responds. Keep the dates visible, but make each date earn a question. Otherwise a team can meet every deadline while its original uncertainty travels untouched into the next quarter.
```

## Scene 2 · 01:40–03:20 · Checkpoint one: diagnose before expanding
**Source:** Ch10, “Make Learning the Only Guaranteed Deliverable,” “Design with Triggers, Not Commitments” (`c013.xhtml`). **Avatar:** off. **B-roll:** synthetic checkpoint card: question, method, owner, review date, possible adjustment; ledger IDs alongside. **Delivery:** Explain what the team will observe and what it will not infer.

**Visible copy:** `Checkpoint 1 · diagnose`.
```narration
Checkpoint one is a diagnosis. The question is whether new members understand the first instruction and the purpose of the task. The method could be a small observation of the starting moment, paired with the original feedback IDs. The owner schedules the review before a larger build begins. If participants can explain the action but cannot tell whether pressing begin worked, confirmation becomes the stronger next question. If they cannot explain what the task is for, the instruction and purpose deserve attention. If both are clear, we reopen the problem instead of forcing one of our planned features through. Write those possible adjustments now. This is what the book means by making learning a deliverable. The team is accountable for running a sound test, reviewing the evidence, and recording a decision. It cannot honestly guarantee the customer outcome before seeing what happens. Keep the method proportionate to the question. A team walkthrough can catch confusing copy before a participant sees it. It cannot tell us how members behave. For that, invite people through a consented research process and keep their notes where your team protects customer information. It is a blueprint for the decision, with enough detail to let another person run the checkpoint.
```

## Scene 3 · 03:20–05:00 · Checkpoint two: test the smaller change
**Source:** Ch10, “Make Learning the Only Guaranteed Deliverable,” “Design with Triggers, Not Commitments” (`c013.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A02, 03:20–03:40. **B-roll:** synthetic first-task card prototype beside baseline version; review card links to L06 trace. **Delivery:** Keep the distinction between a planned threshold and a measured result.

**Visible copy:** `Checkpoint 2 · test the smaller change`.
```narration
Checkpoint two follows only if the first review supports the instruction hypothesis. The team tests the smaller first-task card against the existing version in a planned study. Before observing anyone, define what “clear enough” means. In a moderated task, can the person identify the next action and say why it matters without a staff explanation? What happens when the start action fails? Record the observation method, the number and type of participants the study can responsibly support, and the date the team will decide. The exact threshold is a local design choice for the real test; we will not invent one and call it evidence. On the card, write the condition in plain terms: “If most observed participants still need translation, revise the card and retest before expanding scope.” Also write the alternative: “If they understand the card but do not return, investigate the reminder question.” This is a branch, not a promise that a reminder will work. The roadmap now shows a delivery task and two learning reviews. People can still plan engineering time. They can also see where a result may change that plan. That is more useful than a date that only asks whether the ticket closed.
```

## Scene 4 · 05:00–06:40 · Sam's team changes its question
**Source:** Ch11, “The Moment Sam Knew It Was Working,” “The Questions That Changed Everything,” “What They Stopped Doing” (`c014.xhtml`). **Avatar:** off. **B-roll:** book-story card labeled Sam; three question cards appear in the order the book presents them, no portrait or implied documentary footage. **Delivery:** Distinguish the story from the fictional exercise.

**Visible copy:** `What customer outcome? What will we learn?`.
```narration
Chapter Eleven tells a story about Sam and her team. A junior product manager brings an unfinished idea into the team channel. Sam expects to step in and shape it. Instead, colleagues ask what customer outcome the idea addresses, whether the job has been validated, and what they would learn by testing it. The point of that moment is not that every conversation became perfect. It is that the team had a shared way to examine an idea without waiting for one leader to supply all the judgment. I am using Sam as the book's narrative illustration. That moment belongs to Sam’s story in the book. In the story, the team also stops treating sunk cost as a reason to keep building. That matters because a learning checkpoint only works if the people in the room can act on what they find. If the review date arrives and everyone is afraid to change the plan, the checkpoint is decoration. Put the owner, evidence, and possible adjustment on the roadmap where the team can see them. Make the question normal enough for engineering, design, support, and leadership to use in their own words.
```

## Scene 5 · 06:40–08:20 · A failed test still changes the plan
**Source:** Ch11, “When Tests Failed (And Why That Was Okay),” “What They Stopped Doing” (`c014.xhtml`); Ch10, “Design with Triggers, Not Commitments” (`c013.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A03, 06:40–07:00. **B-roll:** book-case preview-feature card turns into revised search question; no reported percentages or customer proof graphic. **Delivery:** Let the changed question be the payoff.

**Visible copy:** `A failed test can change the next question`.
```narration
The book gives Sam's team a test that does not support the feature they built. They try document previews, expecting people to find the right file faster. The review points them toward a different need: better search. The team records the learning and changes direction. What matters for us is how the team responded when the test challenged its original bet. Look at the sequence. They stated a hypothesis. They chose a signal and review point. They inspected the result. Then they asked what the result meant for the user's job. A low number by itself would not tell them whether the feature was hidden, unwanted, or solving the wrong task. The follow-up conversation mattered. Apply that discipline to our membership example. If the first-task card gets used but members still need staff help, do not declare success because the card was opened. If people never reach it, check the entry path before judging its wording. If the instruction helps but the next step fails, write that down. The team can hold delivery accountable and still let evidence change the next bet. That is a practical promise to the people who have to live with the roadmap.
```

## Scene 6 · 08:20–10:00 · Two dates, two decisions
**Source:** Ch10, “Make Learning the Only Guaranteed Deliverable,” “Design with Triggers, Not Commitments,” “Bottom Line” (`c013.xhtml`); Ch11, “The Questions That Changed Everything” (`c014.xhtml`). **Avatar:** A04, 09:30–10:00. **B-roll:** two-checkpoint roadmap fills beside delivery date; Alignment plate is a brief transition, not a replacement for book fields. **Delivery:** Speak the worksheet fields cleanly; pause after the second decision.

**Visible copy:** `Two checkpoints · two decisions`.
```narration
Pause and choose one delivery milestone from your own plan or from our example. Add two learning checkpoints beside it. For each checkpoint, write the question, the evidence you will use, the owner, the review date, and the adjustment you could make. The first should reduce uncertainty before the team commits too much. The second should inspect the smallest useful test. If a result would never change the plan, rewrite the question until the review has a real consequence. Resume when you can tell a teammate what you will know at each date and what you may do differently. Use information you have permission to share on this worksheet. Keep participant notes in your team’s research system. Your decision file now has a source trail, a living product handoff, and a roadmap that can learn. The two checkpoint cards make the next decision visible to everyone who owns the work. In the next lesson, we will define the measures and write the full learning plan before results arrive. A plan becomes more trustworthy when it shows where it can change. The date is useful because someone has agreed to look, listen, and decide.
```
